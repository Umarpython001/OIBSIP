A life of genius

Alan Mathison Turing (1912–1954) was an English mathematician, computer scientist, logician, cryptanalyst, philosopher, and theoretical biologist. He is widely considered to be the father of theoretical computer science and artificial intelligence. Turing first gained prominence during the Second World War for his work at Bletchley Park, where he played a leading part in breaking the German Enigma ciphers.

Born 23 June 1912, Maida Vale, London. Died 7 June 1954, Wilmslow, Cheshire, aged 41.

His conceptualization of the “Turing machine” provided a formalization of the concepts of algorithm and computation, and showed that a single universal machine could carry out the work of any other. This revolutionary idea became the blueprint for the general-purpose computers we use today. Turing’s ability to bridge the gap between abstract mathematics and practical engineering was unparalleled.

The same 1936 paper settled Hilbert’s Entscheidungsproblem: no mechanical method can decide the truth of every mathematical statement. The machine at the top of this page is its first worked example.

Beyond cryptography and computation, Turing was fascinated by morphogenesis, the process by which biological patterns such as the stripes of a zebra or the spirals of a shell develop. His late-career work in mathematical biology continues to influence the field today, showing a mind that never ceased to explore the fundamental mysteries of nature.

The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 1952. Patterns formed this way are still called Turing patterns.

Despite his monumental contributions to humanity and to the Allied victory, Turing was prosecuted in 1952 for his homosexuality, which was then a crime in the UK. He was posthumously pardoned by Queen Elizabeth II in 2013, and today he is celebrated not only as a scientific titan but as a symbol of resilience and of the fight for human rights.

The British government apologised publicly in 2009. The 2017 “Alan Turing law” extended pardons to thousands of men convicted under the same laws.

In his words

Often misattributed “Sometimes it is the people no one can imagine anything of who do the things no one can imagine” is a line from the 2014 film The Imitation Game, not from Turing’s writing.
